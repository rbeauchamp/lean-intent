# intent-first

Intent-first is a form of intent-driven development in which the owner's intent is written in Lean and the code is proved against it rather than tested.
The method was called spec-first until 2026-11-03, when the owner renamed it intent-first, so records written before then may call it by that name.

A proof shows that code meets its specification, never that the specification says what the owner meant, and a specification that misses the owner's intent is a bug of the same standing as code that misses its specification: this is the premise [lean-intent's README](../../README.md) opens with, and the one this skill serves.
This skill orders the work on one deliverable so that every link below the owner's intent is proved, and the one link no proof reaches, from the owner's words to their first formal statement, stays small, is read a second time blind, and is checked against a restatement of the owner's intent that the owner confirms, before any code exists.

The owner's attention in this method goes to their intent, never to the record's details, by the division between the owner and the agents that [capture-intent](../capture-intent/SKILL.md) states in its section on questions, consequences and unequal minds; this skill applies that division to intent records (step 4).
So a record of the owner's intent describes a system's contract, and a record for an internal function is the agents' design, which the owner does not test or verify, derived from that contract and never presented as the owner's intent.
Below the owner's intent, each property, hypothesis and consequence of a record is the agents' work under that division: derived from the owner's intent and checked against it.

Each part has an owner, and this skill restates none of them:

- [capture-intent](../capture-intent/SKILL.md), published beside this skill, owns how the owner's words become an account of what is wanted, and the division between the owner and the agents; load it by name, or read that file where your tool does not load skills.
- The lean-intent library (https://github.com/rbeauchamp/lean-intent), in whose repository this skill ships, owns the intent record and the proved checks named below; its documentation (the README and, reached from it, the document of each feature under `docs/`) and its module comments are authoritative wherever this skill summarizes them.
- The project you work in owns how its builds and proofs run, where its files go, how it audits axioms, and what counts as evidence.
- No checker requires these results of a project: Regula's issue to enforce them in each project's checks (rbeauchamp/regula#485) was closed as not planned on 2026-10-31, and a project states lean-intent's obligations as theorems of its own.
  The project's build checks each theorem it states, and lean-intent's confirmation check (step 4) checks what it decides; that the project stated and checked every result this skill asks for, nothing but your own report shows.

## Terms

- **Deliverable**: one thing the project promises, such as a function, a step of a system with state, a data format or a value.
- **Subject**: what an intent record is about, a value of a Lean type `W`; an implementation is read as a subject in step 5.
- **Intent record**: a `LeanIntent.IntentRecord W` with four fields: `shape`, what kind of subject it is; `dom`, the named domain hypotheses; `props`, the named properties, each a `LeanIntent.Clause W` (a name and a `W → Prop`); and `vocab`, the predicates the properties are written in.
  `r.Meets w` says every hypothesis and every property of `r` holds of `w`; `r.Fulfilled w` says the properties hold of `w` wherever the hypotheses do.
- **Consequence**: a statement about the record, given in plain words beside the theorem that proves it, of one of three forms: the record requires `P` (`∀ w, r.Meets w → P w`), rules out `x` (`¬ r.Meets x`), or still allows `y` (`r.Meets y`); and the freedom O5 leaves between the subjects that meet it (step 2).
  Steps 2 and 4 add three kinds outside those forms: a trivial witness that meets a pair, beside the theorem that it does; a pair, or the neighbour of a theorem kept for an attack (step 2), left unproved, labelled so; and what an external tool did, beside its measurement instead of a theorem.
  Step 4 gives each consequence a status against the owner's intent: it follows from that intent, it was decided on the owner's behalf, or it is a fork the owner decides.
- **Fork**: a fork in the owner's intent, as [capture-intent](../capture-intent/SKILL.md) defines one in its section on questions, consequences and unequal minds.
  A point that changes only a detail the agents chose, and nothing the owner gets at the level of their intent, is not a fork: by capture-intent's division the owner's intent for such a detail sits one level up, in the purpose it serves, and the agents settle the point against that purpose.
- **Precondition-postcondition pair**: what a property says for each input: the input is whatever the implementation does not choose (an argument, a state, a world from a class), the precondition is what the record demands of that input (a domain hypothesis or a guard inside the property, `True` where it demands nothing), and the postcondition is what the property then asks.
  Every property gives at least one pair; a property of a value with no input gives one whose only input is trivial.
  lean-intent states a pair as a `LeanIntent.Pair`, with its `Input`, `pre` and `post` (`LeanIntent/Pairs.lean`, [docs/pairs.md](../../docs/pairs.md)); `r.PairOf c q` says that `q` is a pair of the property `c` of the record `r`, met by exactly the subjects of which `c` holds wherever the domain hypotheses do; and `Pair.whole r c` is the pair every property has, whose only input is trivial (`pairOf_whole`).
- **Subject that can refuse**: one that the record's source or its type gives an outcome declining or failing the work: an error, a refusal, an incomplete result, a check's rejecting verdict.
  Read this from those two, never from what an implementation does.
  An absent answer that is itself the correct answer, such as a lookup of a missing key, is not one, and a subject with no such outcome takes neither of step 2's two checks for this kind of subject.
- **Act and preparation**: for a subject whose type can perform operations, an act is an operation that sets state a later response can depend on, such as a write, a removal or setting a search path; read this from the cases of its effect type, never from what an implementation calls.
  An operation's preparation is the earlier act its response depends on, such as the copy that a load of the copied file needs.

Add lean-intent to the project's Lake configuration as a dependency pinned to a commit of its `main`, following the project's own rules for dependencies: its README shows the `lakefile.toml` form, and in a `lakefile.lean` it is `require "lean-intent" from git "https://github.com/rbeauchamp/lean-intent" @ "<commit>"`.

## Step 1. From the owner's words to an intent record

1. Apply [capture-intent](../capture-intent/SKILL.md) to the owner's words.
   Its operative account is the record's only source.
   Decide first what the deliverable is: a system's contract, whose record states the owner's intent, or an internal function, whose record is the agents' design, derived from the record of the contract it serves and decided on the owner's behalf; step 4 then restates for the owner that contract, never the internal function's record.
   Keep the owner's words exactly as given beside it, because step 3 works from them and not from your account.
   Start at the level where the owner holds an intent, by capture-intent's division.
   The test is whether the owner has said what the deliverable must do, not whether they named it or whether code for it exists.
   Where they have not, because an agent invented it (a function, a format, a value or anything else), never ask them what it should do, which they could only guess, as Acorn's owner could about `World.dayPhase`.
   Start one level up: find the system's contract or the purpose it serves, asking about that where no statement of it exists, and apply `capture-intent` there.
   The owner's words at that level are then the owner's words for this step and for step 3, and the record's source; derive the record from that contract's record where one exists, and otherwise from the account at that level, and say so in the report.
   Record whose words the source is.
   Words the owner adopted from someone else's proposal (an agent's, a reviewer's, a collaborator's) carry the owner's authority and the proposer's wording, and a proposal is not the owner's until they adopt it (capture-intent's core procedure, step 2).
   Keep beside those words, as an origin statement of the project's owners' log (step 4 item 4, and lean-intent's `docs/owners-log.md`): who wrote them; what that author had read of the implementation and of the work that changed it; the words as adopted; and the owner's adoption, in their own words, with where and when, as the statement's own source and date.
   Never present such a statement as worded by the owner, or as written before the code was seen when its author had read the code.
   Writing the record before reading the implementation does not remove what its source inherited from the code, and step 3 cannot detect it, because the second model receives the same words; say in the report of step 4 that the method's premise is weakened that far.
2. Write the record before you read the implementation.
   Put it in a module of its own that imports the lean-intent modules it uses (`LeanIntent.Record` declares `IntentRecord`) and only what defines the types the subject is stated over, never the module that defines the implementation where the project's layout allows that, and commit it before any implementation is written or changed.
   A value the owner confirmed or an example they named, at this step or in a later answer, is stated as a literal in a property of what the subject decides or produces, which step 5 proves of the actual decision or output, never read from a table or constant the program also reads: a theorem whose expected value comes from the code under proof shows only that code's agreement with itself, and stays true when both sides change.
   This does not reach a value nobody confirmed, which a module the record may import can share with the program; step 2 checks it.
   If you read the implementation before writing the record, say so in the report of step 4.
3. Write each requirement of the account as a named property, with a name the owner can read.
   Write each fact about the world that those properties rely on as a named domain hypothesis, never as a premise hidden inside a property; step 4 checks each against the owner's intent and the evidence for it.
   Put each predicate the properties use in `vocab`.
4. Choose the shape by what the subject is.
   Use `.function` for a function whose result depends only on its arguments; for a subject that carries state from one call to the next, the step constructor its type has (`.step`, `.stepOn`, `.action` or `.actionOn`); for a description made of functions that carries no state from one use to the next, such as a world's rules, `.bundle`, which names each function and the plain values that are the rest of the subject (lean-intent's `docs/obligations.md`); and `.value` only for a value with no behaviour.
   Never declare a subject with state as `.function` or `.value`, or a description made of functions as `.value`: O4 then asks of it only totality, or nothing, and a subject that does nothing passes (lean-intent's document of the intent record, `docs/intent-record.md`, lists the shape among what is trusted).

## Step 2. Prove the record adequate

Prove each obligation as a theorem in the project, checked by the project's build with no `sorry` and audited as the project audits axioms.
Each obligation `Oi r` is the conjunction of the goals its former computes, and its correctness theorem (`o2_correct` and the rest) rewrites it to the intended proposition, which is usually the easier form to prove; lean-intent's `LeanIntentExamples/Sorting.lean` proves every one of them for one record.

- **O1, well formed**: holds of every record by construction (`o1_wellTyped`), so there is nothing to prove.
- **O2, satisfiable**: prove `O2 r`, by `o2_correct` a joint model of the domain hypotheses and an instance of each predicate of the vocabulary.
- **O2 before running**: `O2Run r` asks for a subject that meets the whole record.
  Prove it now where you can exhibit one; otherwise the implementation supplies it in step 5 (`Refines.o2Run`).
  For a subject that can refuse it is due now: the first model of what a healthy input owes, below, proves it.
- **O3, refutable**: prove `O3 r`, by `o3_correct` a subject that fails each property.
  Each such subject is a "rules out" consequence for step 4.
- **O4, progress**: prove `O4 r`.
  For a function, `o4_function r rfl` proves it; for a step, `o4_correct` asks that every subject that leaves the state unchanged fail the record; for a `.bundle`, that its functions and plain values make up the whole subject, that each function is free to be any function of its type, and that every subject in which one of its functions ignores its input fail the record.
  A record that a subject doing nothing meets fails O4 (`not_o4_of_idle`); revise it.
- **O5, determinate or confirmed freedom**: first try `O5 r .eq`, that any two subjects meeting the record are equal.
  Where two different subjects meet it (`not_o5_eq` then refutes `.eq`), find the relation the record leaves open between them and prove `O5 r (.rel R)`; `O5 r .widest` always holds (`o5_widest`) and says the record leaves every choice between its models open.
  Whichever you prove, the freedom is a consequence for step 4: O5 never refuses a record by itself; it makes the freedom a consequence that step 4 checks against the owner's intent.
- `progress` proves O1 to O4, and O5 at `.widest`, from five premises: a model of the domain hypotheses, an instance of each predicate, a subject failing each property, a sound shape (`Shape.Sound`, which asks something only of a `.bundle`), and no subject doing nothing that meets the record. Still try a narrower freedom than `.widest`.
- **Independence from the implementation**: whenever the implementation exists, here and again in step 5, run lean-intent's cone exclusion on the record, `LeanIntent.Cone.requireIndependent`, whose `checkRecord_eq_ok_iff` states what it decides (`docs/cone-exclusion.md`).
  Name every declaration the implementation consists of, its helpers and every member of a block of mutually recursive definitions included, and run it where every module is imported in full, such as a file outside Lean's module system, as that document says.
  A property that restates the implementation's body without naming it passes that check; step 4 checks it against the owner's intent as the consequence "the output is computed exactly as this expression".
- **Degenerate implementations**: prove the verdicts of lean-intent's degenerate-subject check, `Verdicts r bs` (`docs/degenerate-subjects.md`), which state of each member of the class `LeanIntent.degenerate` fixes by the subject's type either that a subject it stands for meets the record or that none does.
  Where the subject's type is one that check does not cover, which Lean reports by failing to synthesize `Covered`, take instead the degenerate subjects that the interim class in [references/interim-checks.md](references/interim-checks.md) fixes by the subject's type, and prove of each that it fails the record (`¬ r.Meets d`) or that it meets it (`r.Meets d`).
  Each one that meets the record is a consequence for step 4; for the check, `possibilities` reports them.
- **Values shared with the program**: once a program exists, list from Lean's environment each definition of the project that takes no arguments and is no type, and that both the program and the record or a theorem about it reach, following every constant in each declaration's type and value, proofs included, as Lean's `Lean.ConstantInfo.getUsedConstantsAsSet` reports them.
  In a scratch copy, replace each in turn, once, with a different constant of its type (an empty list, an empty string), and rebuild.
  A value survives when no theorem stating a property fails, which means only that the rebuilt properties did not exclude the chosen replacement: state a literal property over the decision or output, or name it as accepted risk with its reason, except that a value the owner confirmed takes the literal property (step 1).
  A failure shows only that some property reads the value, never that each of its entries is constrained.
  This is a diagnostic: keep its result for the report and nothing of it in the repository.
  A mutation analysis of a Lean project found the owner's confirmed examples written as membership in a module of tables the program read, and tables of that module, replaced by a constant, still built.
- **What a predicate's description claims**: a published description of a predicate (a docstring, a document, a message the program prints, the predicate's own name) claims only what its inputs can establish.
  A form is not an origin: where two inputs of one form have different origins, the predicate gives them one verdict, so word the description as the form, or give the predicate the origin as an input.
- **Every pair can be met for every input it admits**: for each pair, prove that the precondition admits an input and that each input it admits has an implementation meeting the postcondition: `(∃ x, Pre x) ∧ ∀ x, Pre x → ∃ i, Post x i`.
  lean-intent's pair obligation is exactly these two conjuncts, with the implementation drawn from the permitted implementation class `C` (`fun _ => True` where every subject of the record's type is permitted): state the pair as a `LeanIntent.Pair` `q`, prove it a pair of its property (`r.PairOf c q`) or take `Pair.whole r c`, and prove `OPair q C`, which `oPair_correct` equates, for every pair and every class, with `(∃ x, q.pre x) ∧ ∀ x, q.pre x → ∃ w, C w ∧ q.post x w` (`LeanIntent/Pairs.lean`, [docs/pairs.md](../../docs/pairs.md)).
  This is due now, whether or not O2 before running waits for step 5, and neither O2 form replaces it: `O2 r` asks only for a model of the hypotheses and the vocabulary, and `O2Run r` for one subject, which, where the subject's type carries the input (a world inside the subject), fixes one convenient input.
  A proof of `O2Run r` discharges a pair only when its one subject serves every input the pair admits; and this check does not replace `O2Run r`, because it does not show that one implementation meets every pair.
  An input no implementation can serve means the precondition is too wide, so treat it as a failed obligation (`not_oPair_of_unserved`): in rbeauchamp/acorn PR 190 a proposed class of worlds admitted two worlds with identical percepts that required opposite actions, which no agent can tell apart.
  A pair you can neither prove nor refute is a consequence for step 4 labelled unproved, never a pass.
- **No pair is met by a trivial witness**: the check above lets the implementation depend on the input, so it says nothing about whether a pair asks for the work it is meant to ask for. For a pair whose subject type lean-intent's codes cover, its trivial-witness check states a closed class of such witnesses fixed by that type, with a verdict to prove on each (`WitnessVerdicts q bs`, `docs/trivial-witnesses.md`), a theorem committed where the project's proof check builds it (Throughout, item 7); it covers no other type (rbeauchamp/lean-intent#43), and it holds a script of timed actions only on a clock its class reads as one (the document's section on clocks): the value of a component of an argument, of any covered type, reached through pairs and the fields of declared types whose values all have one shape, such as structures and, in a recursive, nested or mutual type, a type of its group with one constructor that is not the group's root, such as a rose tree's label and children, or such a component's list length, array size or, for a recursive type shaped as a list, such as a history type a project declares with `nil` and `cons`, its length, whose reading does not determine the arguments, or a part typed `LeanIntent.Clock α`. Where a subject's clock is all it reads, such as a policy of type `Nat → Act` whose number is the time, state its pairs over a type that gives that part the type `LeanIntent.Clock α`, such as `Clock Nat → Act`, or its scripts are not reported. The class leaves other witnesses out, so the attack below still applies to each pair.
  For each pair, take the account's reason for it and construct the cheapest implementation that meets it without doing that work; try at least one that fixes its answers to the input in advance (a table, or a script of timed actions), one that ignores the input, and one that meets the postcondition only because the precondition fails or the postcondition holds emptily.
  Prove of each that it fails the pair or that it meets it; the degenerate subjects above are fixed by the subject's type and do not stand in for this attack, which is made against each pair.
  Each witness that meets a pair is a consequence for step 4: strengthen the pair where the account rules the witness out, for example by asking one implementation to serve a whole class of inputs, and otherwise keep it as what the record allows.
  Where the permitted implementation class is closed under choosing independently at each input, and each postcondition reads the implementation only at its own input, the first check's witnesses glue into a table that meets the pair.
  Establish that closure, including any computability and resource constraints, before claiming a table witness; input-local postconditions alone do not establish it.
  An admitted table is no defect where the account asks only for that input-output behaviour.
  A claim that some kind of implementation cannot meet a pair is a universal negative: state it as a theorem or delete it.
  In rbeauchamp/acorn PR 190 the claim that no agent without learning can reach the goals was false, because a timed script solves every feasible goal, and the contract had to be restated across a class of worlds.
- **The trivial-witness attack on a subject that can refuse**: the witnesses above ignore the input or fix its answers, so a subject that does the work and adds a reason of its own escapes them.
  Add three: one that performs an extra operation the record's source does not name and reports its failure as the reason; one that reads what it needs, gets a good answer and refuses anyway; and one that asks a needed operation before the operation that prepares it and reports the failure it caused.
  Choose the extra operation, and the third subject's operation and preparation, from the source, never from the code; where the record names an operation its source does not, attack with that one too, because it came from the code or from a choice step 4 must see.
  Where the subject's type can perform no operation, the extra operation is a test of the input that the source does not name, and the third subject does not exist, because it has no earlier act; where the source and the effect type's cases give no needed operation both a preparation and a way to fail, there is no third subject either; the report says which, and why.
  Prove of each that it fails the record (`¬ r.Meets d`) or that it meets it (`r.Meets d`), which is a consequence for step 4.
  In rbeauchamp/regula PR 521 the record of the whole run accepted an error on any failed event, and `Event.failedLoad` counted every absent environment variable as one, so a program that read an unrelated variable and reported its absence met the record.
  A theorem kept for an attack or named for one, here or below, must hold for the attack's own reason, not because the subject lacks something unrelated.
  So give the subject everything the attack does not touch, every answer included, as a run the record admits would have it, and prove its neighbour meets what the theorem refutes, for at least one instance: the same subject with only the attack's own act or reason replaced by what the record accepts.
  Where the neighbour fails too, the theorem says nothing of the attack, so restate it; a neighbour you can neither prove nor refute is a consequence for step 4 labelled unproved, never a pass.
  In rbeauchamp/regula PR 522 `manufacturedCopy_fails` held through an absent `LEAN_SYSROOT` answer, because `FullRun.no_sysroot_no_copy` then forbids every copy, so the trace failed with or without the attack's act; `manufacturedCopyAnswered_fails` had to state the attack with that answer present.
- **The subject's own acts**: a subject whose type can perform no operation, such as a record of pure functions, has no effects and takes none of this check.
  Every other subject takes its last three parts, preservation, what the acts leave and the role of what they leave, however its record reads them; a record whose properties read the operations a run performed also takes the grammar and the attack by an act that come first.
  Membership of an event in the trace can say an operation is relevant, never that it was prepared or that its preparation still stands.
  So state the acts as a closed ordered grammar: a definition of the run's whole sequence of acts that fixes each act's place and arguments, from the source and earlier responses, covers every failed attempt and early stop that the source or the effect type allows, and admits nothing else; where the effect type has no closed set of cases, close it first.
  Reads stay free between acts, except that an operation whose response depends on an act comes after that act, even if it changes nothing.
  Derive the grammar from what the source needs, never from the implementation's calls, and give step 4 each order it fixes that the source does not require.
  Where the record's source or the cases of the effect type let an act fail, read from those and never from an implementation, prove a model of a run in which one fails for a reason outside the run, so that the grammar, like the first model of what a healthy input owes (below), admits what the source needs; otherwise the report says that no act can fail, and why.
  Attack it with an act the run itself performs to make a later operation fail: from each act case of the effect type, at each place and with each argument the grammar leaves free, whether it leaves out a preparation, undoes it, obstructs its target or otherwise, with the run reporting that failure.
  Prove of each that it fails the record or that it meets it, which is a consequence for step 4; one theorem over every trace the grammar admits covers a whole case, so prefer it.
  A subject the account rules out that meets the record changes the grammar, never only the clause that admitted it; run the whole attack again.
  In rbeauchamp/regula PR 522 the record bound each operation to the build by membership, so a load before its copies, a removal before a build and a directory made at a copy's destination each met it, and repairing one at a time left the next; the ordered grammar of `ReadingPreparation`, `BuildProtocol` and the definitions beside them closed the class.
  Attack preservation: a grammar says which acts may occur and never what they leave, and the attack above tests a later operation that fails, never a later act that changes what an earlier judgement was about while the old evidence stays credited.
  From the cases of the effect type, closed first where they are not, and each predicate of the record that reads an event, list each fact a verdict or other result rests on about state the subject's own acts can change; where there is none, the report says why.
  For each fact, name its consumer, the operation or outside reader that relies on that state, and state the fact as a function of the acts up to the consumer, never as membership of an event.
  Attack it with an act of each case of the effect type that changes that state, after the fact was established and before the consumer, for every value of the act's arguments and whatever the act answers, with the earlier evidence kept; name each other case with the reason it cannot change that state.
  Each run whose act leaves the fact false fails the record, proved over all such values, or its meeting the record is a consequence for step 4; the neighbour that a theorem kept for an attack needs (above) is here the same act leaving the fact true.
  A record that still credits the earlier evidence misses the fact, so revise it; a lemma that a fact keeps under more events is the sign to look for, because it holds only while the state the fact is about stays fixed.
  In rbeauchamp/regula PR 523 `ReadAs` read a copy as events of the trace and `Evidence.mono` kept them, so the record credited a text after a later copy replaced it, a trace the grammar admits (`moduleCopies_admits_uncheckedCopy`); `Evidence` over `written`, what the run's writes and removals leave, gave `uncheckedCopy_fails`, with `judgedCopy_stands` as its neighbour.
  Beside that attack, state what the acts leave by what the external system answers after the subject's last act, not only by a fold of its requests.
  A fold is a model of that system, and each assumption it needs (that two keys name two objects, that a success stored what it was sent, that a failure left what stood, that another case changes nothing) is an external hypothesis the subject can observe instead.
  So the record has the subject read that state after its last act on it and before the consumer, through the name the consumer will use, and credits the answer; attack that too with each act after the read, and name as an external hypothesis what is left, that the answer still holds at the consumer.
  An assumption stays only where the subject cannot observe it: the report says why, and it takes the check of an external hypothesis below, over the run's requests and responses; listing in the trusted base what one more read would observe is not enough.
  Attack the statement with every aliasing the system applies, read from its documentation or source at the version in use, such as two names for one object by case folding, normalization or a link: a run in which one request replaces what another left, under another name, fails the record, or its meeting the record is a consequence for step 4.
  In rbeauchamp/regula PR 523 the first repair stated what stands as a fold keyed by path components, with "two keys name two files" and "a success stored the text" in the trusted base; on a case-folding file system, with a file swapped between its two reads, the actual checker gave a full pass with only the second text in the tree, though each text had been judged only under its own name, until the run read each planned file back after its last act and `ReadBack` credited what the file system answered.

1. Seal your own first: commit the record, its obligations and step 2's statement of `Healthy` before you read anything the second model writes.
2. Use a model from a different provider than the one that wrote your record, of similar capability, so that it shares fewer of your misreadings. 3. Its only source of what is wanted is the owner's words, exactly as given.
   It also receives what it needs to write Lean, none of which says what is wanted: the subject's type where the owner's words or the existing code fix it, the source of the definitions of the types the subject is stated over, and lean-intent's `LeanIntent/Record.lean`.

   For cases it also receives the input's own format from that format's owner, which says what an input is and not what is wanted, and it needs no `Record.lean`.

## Step 4. The owner confirms their intent, and the agents check the record against it

This is the method's one check of the link from the owner's intent to the record, and the owner made it a precondition of any code.
It divides the work by capture-intent's division: the owner confirms a plain restatement of their intent and decides each fork, one-way door and trade of quality for cost; the agents check every consequence of the record against what the owner confirmed and decide the rest on the owner's behalf.
Reach the owner through the channel your instructions give you for the owner's decisions, and ask as [capture-intent](../capture-intent/SKILL.md) says: a question about what is wanted, never a request to approve a step or a detail of the record.

Before item 1 asks anything, do these two things; the restatement carries their results.

**Measure each consequence that rests on an external tool.**
A consequence rests on an external tool when its truth depends on how something outside the project's proofs behaves on this project, such as a build tool's command, a checker, a linter or a service.
Run that tool once on the first project the specification applies to, the way the consequence says it will run and under that project's rules for heavy work, and record the tool's version, the configuration, the result and what it cost in time and memory, as a measurement (Throughout, item 4).
This is not implementing the deliverable, so the rule that closes this step does not hold it back.
A refusal, a cost nobody measured, or a consequence measured only on a stand-in for that project is named as open in the restatement the owner confirms, in plain words, with what depends on it; where the project cannot yet run the tool because the deliverable does not exist, measure what it can run and name the rest.
In rbeauchamp/regula PR 505 `lake check` refused the project's three top-level `main` declarations, which the confirmed specification had not foreseen; one run before confirmation would have shown it.
One run is evidence for the consequence on that project, not a bound and not a proof.
Where no consequence rests on such a tool, say so in the report.

**Write the plain words last, from the finished predicates.**
Plain words about what the record says, wherever they will appear (a consequence, the restatement, a pull request description, a document, a docstring), are written only once the revision they describe has been through steps 2 and 3 as far as item 6 requires, and are read off that revision's predicates and theorems, never carried over from the account, an earlier draft or what a predicate was meant to say.
Check each sentence quantifier by quantifier against the predicate: write out its quantifiers in order, with the domain and hypotheses of each (every, some, none, at most, eventually; "for each goal some agent" against "one agent for every goal"), and compare them with the predicate's binders in order.
A sentence that claims more, less or a different order is wrong: rewrite it, or, where the account requires what it says, revise the record (item 6).
In rbeauchamp/acorn PR 190 the plain contract promised eventual achievement in any member of a class while the predicate asked only for some set of members larger than a bound, so an agent that abandoned a member forever still met it.
Item 1's restatement still states the owner's intent, from the account, so compare it in both directions: each requirement of the account appears in it, and each clause that says what the record requires is read from a predicate.
A requirement the finished record does not carry is a revision (item 6), or is named in the restatement as not yet promised.
Where the deliverable is internal and its contract has no record yet, the restatement speaks from the account and claims nothing about a record, while each consequence's plain words still come from the internal record's predicates.
A revision makes the plain words of whatever it changed stale, so derive them again.

1. Restate the owner's intent in plain words, at the level they hold it: the account of step 1, with examples and with what it leaves unsaid.
   By capture-intent's division that intent is a system's contract, never the specification of an internal function: for an internal function, or any other deliverable an agent invented, restate the contract or purpose it serves (the system's contract, the project's purpose, or a statement the owner adopted), not the deliverable's details.
   Ask the owner to confirm the restatement or correct it; where the owner has already confirmed this same restatement, unchanged, cite where and when instead of asking again.
2. Check each consequence of the record against the confirmed restatement: what it requires (each property, what a healthy input owes, with its premises, `Healthy` itself and what it leaves out, how each refusal was related to it, each order a grammar of acts fixes that the source does not require, each fact stated at its consumer, and each name the subject asks through after its last act), what it rules out (the subjects of O3 and the degenerate subjects it refutes), what it still allows (the degenerate subjects that meet it, each subject of the attack on a subject that can refuse or on a grammar of acts that meets it, each run of the attack on preservation or by an aliasing that meets it, each file judged in no role that a name reaches and that still meets it, each subject that splices a value as it stands and still meets it, and two different subjects that meet it where O5 is not `.eq`), the freedom O5 leaves, each domain hypothesis, each difference step 3 found, and each assumption that remains (the shape, each interim check in use, a dependency the cone check could not exclude, each external hypothesis a theorem takes, each assumption a fold of requests keeps, each reader the attack on role left out, each shared value left as accepted risk, each part step 3 could not compare).
   Cite the theorem behind each consequence; the plain words themselves are trusted, not proved.
   Check the same way each trivial witness that meets a pair, each pair left unproved and each neighbour of a theorem kept for an attack left unproved (step 2), and each measurement and open item from above, citing the measurement where no theorem stands behind it.

3. Prove the definitions that execute, as the project requires.
   Where the implementation already exists, prove it as it stands; a property it fails is a finding, either a bug in the code or a consequence that step 4 gives a status (and brings to the owner when its status is for the owner), and never a reason to weaken the record.
4. The link carries the record's guarantees to the image of `m`, not to the implementations themselves (`Refines.determinate`, `Refines.refutes`, and lean-intent's `LeanIntentExamples/Transfer.lean`).

6.    The sentence-by-sentence check of step 4 does not replace this pass: in rbeauchamp/regula PR 522, 16 of the 105 theorem docstrings the change added said more than their statements.
   The author's own pass there still left a comment on `copyFile_io` promising a write below the target, for a statement with no such premise, and `copyFile` with an absolute path wrote outside it; in PR 523 readers who had not written them corrected about 80 sentences across about 730 comments.
2. Each data format has one parser, and that parser is proved against the format's specification; two readers of one format meet one specification, each proved against it or derived from it.
3. Never write a scanner or reader by hand for an open language such as JavaScript: read a closed format the project defines, refusing everything outside it, or use the owning tool's own parser.
   rbeauchamp/acorn#165 shows why: a hand-written JavaScript scanner in a gate hid executable code from the gate and exposed literal text to it as code, and three independent readings of one change each found more gaps of that kind.
4. Every claim says how it is established: proved (name the theorem), measured (with the run and its configuration), argued, assumed or open.
5. A check or command cited as the guard of a property is evidence for it only through a theorem about its own decision, and a green run of it is an observation of one input.
   So name that theorem in the citation, and read its statement against the property as the citation words it: a theorem about an inner function, or about a model with fixed callbacks or in another monad, covers only that, unless a further theorem links it to what the command decides when it runs.
   Where no such theorem exists, do not cite the check as a guard: report its runs as measured and the property as assumed or open.
   An audit of Regula found its specification citing three commands as covering programs kept for validation, when what stood behind them was a theorem in one state monad with two fixed callbacks, a theorem about an inner function, and a command with no theorem; the checks ran and passed, and that read as coverage.
6. A change that adds or changes an unproved assumption about an external boundary gives that assumption exactly one disposition, in its pull request where it states its trusted base.
   An external boundary is where a claim of the work rests on how something its proofs do not reach behaves, such as foreign code (C, `extern`, `implemented_by`), compiled code where the proof is about the kernel's logic, or a reader of another tool's output or of the operating system; these are examples, not a closed list.
   A change also changes such an assumption when it changes what is on the other side of the boundary, such as an upgrade of a toolchain or dependency on that side, even where no statement of the assumption changes.
   The dispositions are three: a proof that removes the assumption; a time-limited counterexample search, differential against the real system, which gives the real system and what the assumption says of it the same inputs and compares their answers, with its budget (at most about an hour of machine time) and its stop rule (the first counterexample) stated before it runs, and its result; or accepted risk, with its reason.
   A change that adds or changes no such assumption owes none of this, so the search is never a step every change takes.
   The search is empirical evaluation, which this method reserves for questions derivation cannot answer: before it runs, say why derivation cannot answer the claim, and define the smallest informative search, its decision criteria, its budget and how its uncertainty is treated; difficulty proving something is an unresolved proof obligation, not evidence that the question is empirical; and measurements, sampled agreement, coverage percentages and audit digests do not establish universal correctness.
   So it never stands in for a proof where one can remove the assumption, and a search that finds no counterexample is a measurement of the runs it made (item 4 above), which leaves the assumption assumed.
   A counterexample it finds is fixed for its whole class, never only as that instance, and is never kept as a test.
   This disposition is owed beside the checks the steps above give such an assumption, and replaces none of them.
7. Every theorem this skill has you prove, such as an obligation, a consequence, a comparison of step 3, a kept answer or the proof that the implementation fulfils the record, is committed in the project in a module that its proof check builds, so that every run of that check checks it.
   Committing is not enough: a check that builds declared targets skips a committed module that none of its targets reaches, and a theorem that nothing builds is checked by nothing, however finished its step reads.
   For each module that holds such theorems, confirm from the build tool's own account of what the check builds that it builds that module, as [the reference's section on where step 3's results live](references/blind-formalization.md#where-the-formalization-and-the-comparison-live) does for step 3's modules, and keep that account for the report.
